# The through-line: the world has changed

## Executive summary

**What this is.** The one idea that runs through every workshop in this kit, written so a graduate student facilitator can say it in their own words in two minutes.

**Why read it.** The easy version of this day is "here is some cool AI, now brainstorm." That would waste twenty smart undergraduates. The world has changed, and these students are graduating into it. Two things are true at once, and the day must teach both:

1. **Master the tools.** AI now drafts, summarizes, codes, designs and plans in seconds. A student who cannot direct it well is already behind.
2. **Keep what is irreducibly human.** The cheap part is now producing a first draft. The hard parts that remain are choosing the right problem, smelling a wrong answer, being with people, deciding together, and owning the consequence. No tool does those for you.

**What it decides.**

- Every workshop has a **Master it** half and a **Keep it human** half. Neither is optional.
- Students never watch AI from the back of the room. A student's hands are on the keyboard, and they direct the AI the way a driver directs a car.
- Every group ends with a **Driver's Ledger**: what the AI did, what we checked, what a human decided and signed, and why that last part stays human.
- Pitches are scored on both: did you make something real with AI, and did you keep your own judgment.

**What it does not claim.** The "world has changed" idea is a teaching thesis, not a measured law. We do not quote adoption or productivity statistics, and we never say "AI can never do X." See [Lens, not law](#lens-not-law).

## The world has changed (what we can show, not just say)

In the first minutes a student, not the presenter, gives a general-purpose AI a real task and the room watches it build something that runs. No slides about "disruption." Just the thing happening.

Then one question to the room: **if the first draft is now free, what is left for you?**

That question is the whole day. The rest is how to answer it.

## Commitment 1: Master the tools

Four moves. Students practice all four in every lab.

| Move | In plain words | What the student does |
|---|---|---|
| **Spec it** | Say precisely what you want, and what you do not | Write three lists before prompting: **must match**, **may decide**, **must stop and ask me** |
| **Direct it** | You are directing, not typing | Loop: frame, plan, draft, audit, revise. One person drives the keyboard while the group directs |
| **Check it** | Fluent is not the same as true | Before looking anything up, write what a wrong answer would look like. Check with something that fails *differently* than the AI (a source, a person, a calculation), not with the same AI |
| **Chain it** | Skills and agents are written procedures with gates | Read a real skill or agent like a contract. Find the steps where it must stop and hand back to a human |

**Match your scrutiny to your use.** Three levels, as a lens: Level 1 is a copy editor (a glance is enough), Level 2 is a research assistant (check against sources), Level 3 is a collaborator that shapes how you frame the problem (supervise hard). The risk is the gap between how you use it and how closely you check it.

## Commitment 2: Keep what is irreducibly human

Five human moves, in undergraduate words. Each maps to a capacity the author's framework (see [Lens, not law](#lens-not-law)) places at the human end of the work.

| Human move | In plain words | Where it shows up in the day |
|---|---|---|
| **Notice the right problem** | Choose which problem is worth solving, and frame it so different framings would need different fixes | Every track's spec step; the web app (what does the game count as a win?) |
| **Smell a wrong answer** | Judge whether an output could even be true *before* you look it up | The intro's catch round, every track's Catch Card |
| **Be with people** | Care, consent, trust, being present for someone frightened or stuck | The agent (who must a person handle?), the game (watching a stranger play) |
| **Disagree and decide together** | Hold your own position, argue, and reach a decision a group will own | The group's own build, the demo round |
| **Own the consequence** | Put your name on it; accept what happens next | The signature line on every Driver's Ledger |

Three verdicts for any task, as a way to think about handing work over:

- **Hand it over.** Pattern-finding, formatting, retrieval, first drafts. Do it gladly. *Handing over is not deleting:* you cannot check what you never learned.
- **Supervise it.** The AI helps, a human reads every line and can override.
- **Protect it.** Framing the problem, working out cause and effect, deciding among values, being accountable. The student does this, and the AI at most advises.

The skill of sorting a task into one of these three is itself one of the human skills.

**Assign by task, never by pride.** We never say "humans are smarter." We say: *this task needs someone who can be held accountable, so a person does it.*

## The Driver's Ledger

One tool, used in every track, printed on the take-home card. Full template in [workshops/06](workshops/06-the-ledger-and-the-demos.md).

| What the AI did | What we checked, and how | What a human decided and signs | Why that part stays human |
|---|---|---|---|

"Looks good" is not an entry. A check names *what* was checked and *against what*. A sign-off names *who*. And every ledger ends with one honest line: **what we did not check.**

## Lens, not law

These ideas come from the author's own teaching frameworks (Nik Bear Brown). They are useful lenses. They are not research findings. Facilitators say "here is a way to think about it," not "science shows."

**Never say or show:**

- Any adoption, productivity or job-loss statistic. The kit does not use them. The ones circulating are contested, and some we checked were wrong.
- "AI can never ..." or "AI cannot feel/understand." Say what we actually saw it do or not do in our own demo.
- "Humans are smarter than AI." Say what task needs accountability.
- That the three levels, the tiers, or the ledger are standard industry terms. They are the author's teaching tools.
- Anything about a student's grade, major or background.

**Always do:** if a demo goes wrong, that is the lesson. Say what happened. Do not rerun it quietly until it works.
