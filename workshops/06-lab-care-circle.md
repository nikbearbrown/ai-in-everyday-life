# 06. Lab: Care Circle

*Status: DRAFT, not yet dry-run. Small group, 40 minutes. Topic: AI supporting healthcare providers and patients.*

## Executive summary

**What this is.** A group designs a tool that helps a patient get ready for a visit, understand an instruction, or helps a provider with paperwork. It never gives medical advice. **Why run it.** Healthcare is the sharpest test of the through-line. AI can draft, translate and summarize at speed; the human parts (consent, accountability, being present for someone frightened, deciding when to say "I don't know") are the whole job. The human skills this lab trains are **own the consequence** and **being with people.** The tool skill is **Check it.** **What the group leaves with.** A plain-language handout, an "I don't know" list, a privacy line, a Catch Card, and a Driver's Ledger.

| | |
|---|---|
| **Time** | 40 minutes |
| **Group** | 4 students plus one coach |
| **Materials** | Driver's Canvas, scenario cards, one shared laptop with a general-purpose AI |
| **Tech** | Wi-Fi for AI. Paper fallback below. |

## Safety rules (read aloud at the start)

1. **No real patient information, ever.** All scenarios are made up. No student shares their own health information.
2. **The tool does not diagnose, prescribe or advise treatment.** It prepares, explains and organizes. A clinician decides.
3. **If a student shares something personal, the coach redirects kindly.** Offer the campus resources the UConn host provides. The coach is not a counselor.

## The two jobs in this lab

- **Master it:** Check it. Verify any health-related statement against a trusted independent source before it goes in the handout. Never ask the AI to confirm itself.
- **Keep it human:** Own the consequence. If the handout is wrong, someone is hurt, so a person with the right authority must sign. Design "I don't know" and "ask your clinician" as valid answers.

## Scenario cards (deal one)

A patient preparing for a first appointment. A patient leaving a clinic with instructions in medical language. An older patient who needs a simple reminder routine. A clinic front desk drowning in scheduling messages. A nurse who spends an hour on paperwork each shift.

## Run of show

1. **Meet the person (6 min). User hat.** Write a short portrait. What are they afraid of? What do they not know to ask? What would feel disrespectful?
2. **Spec it (8 min). Data hat.** Must match: plain language, accurate, no new medical claims. May decide: layout, order, examples. Must stop and hand to a clinician: symptoms, doses, any emergency, any "should I." Name what data the tool would touch and say who is allowed to see it.
3. **Direct it (12 min).** A student drives. Build a one-page handout and a short list of five questions to ask the clinician. Then ask the AI to read the handout back to a ten-year-old.
4. **Check it (8 min). Skeptic hat.** For every health claim in the handout, tag it **verified** (checked against a named trusted source), **unverified,** or **remove.** The coach may supply a trusted source; the group may not use the same AI to verify. Make a **"we do not know" list.**
5. **Driver's Ledger and pitch outline (6 min).** Fill it in. The line that matters: **who signs this before a patient sees it, and what are they signing?**

## Debrief questions

- Which sentence in the handout would worry you if it were wrong?
- Where did the AI sound surest and deserve it least?
- What did you leave to a person on purpose?

## Unplugged fallback

The coach plays the AI. Hand the group a handout with two planted, plausible errors in it. They must find and label them using the coach's reference sheet.

## Watch-outs

- Never present the handout as real guidance. It is a design exercise.
- If the AI gives a confident dose or diagnosis, stop. That is the lesson. Do not use it.
- Respect the group. People bring real experiences. Keep the exercise about the design.
