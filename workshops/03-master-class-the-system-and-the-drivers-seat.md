# 03. The System and the Driver's Seat

*Status: DRAFT, not yet dry-run. Master class, whole room.*

## Executive summary

**What this is.** A 10-minute close to the master class that shows (a) what an *information system* is, using an app every student knows, and (b) how real AI skills and agents are written down as procedures with places where a human must step in. **Why run it.** It names what SEIS actually teaches. AI is rarely the whole product. It sits inside a system of people, data, process and rules, and the professional's job is to design that system so AI is useful and a human stays accountable. It also teaches the fourth tool skill, **Chain it.** **What students leave with.** The four hats they will wear in the lab (User, Data, Process, Skeptic) and the Driver's Ledger.

## The two jobs in this module

- **Master it:** Chain it. Read a real skill and a real agent like a contract.
- **Keep it human:** Find every point where the procedure must stop and hand back to a person, and say why.

## Prep (before the day)

1. Pick **one skill** to print from the author's public skills library (bearbrown.co/skills). Choose a readable one such as the Socratic prompt coach or the venture pitch skill. Print the first page.
2. Pick **one agent** from the library at bearbrown.co/agents. Choose a readable one with a short purpose, for example an email triage or an invoice-processing workflow. Print its description and its list of steps. **Say honestly that it is a source workflow and has not been run in our test.**
3. Prepare the "four hats" poster and print the Driver's Ledger (see [09](09-shared-drivers-canvas-and-ethics-cards.md)).

## Run of show

1. **Anatomy of a system (3 min).** Pick an app everyone uses (a food delivery app, a ride app, a campus portal). Draw four boxes on the board: **People** (who uses it and who is affected), **Data** (what it knows), **Process** (what happens, step by step), **Technology** (including any AI). Ask: *where would AI go? What happens when it is wrong? Who finds out?* The answer is almost always a person, a process or a rule that catches it.
2. **Read a skill like a contract (3 min).** Groups mark up the printed skill. Circle every phase gate. Underline every rule that says **stop and ask the human**. Ask: *what is it protecting?*
3. **Read an agent like a job description (2 min).** Same exercise on the agent. For each step, mark **machine** or **human**. Ask: *what would go wrong if the human step were removed?*
4. **The four hats and the ledger (2 min).** Hand out the Driver's Ledger. Say: "In the lab you wear four hats. **User**: who is hurt and what do they need? **Data**: what do we know and what are we missing? **Process**: what happens today and where does it break? **Skeptic**: what could go wrong and how would we know? And you fill in the ledger at the end."

## Say this out loud

"A good agent is not a robot that replaces a person. It is a procedure that says exactly where it stops and a person takes over. Designing that line is the job. It is one of the main things a degree in information systems teaches."

## Facilitator notes

- Say what the agent printout is: *a workflow someone collected, not proof it works.* This is itself the lesson.
- The good rule to quote: **AI executes; humans decide.** A gate is a *specific, testable condition cleared by a named person*. "Looks good" is not a gate.
- If you have time, open the live agents page and let a student click through one workflow.

## Unplugged fallback

Everything here works on printouts.

## Watch-outs

- Do not oversell the agents library. It is a library of workflows, most of them untested.
- Do not teach the four hats as an industry standard. It is a classroom device.
