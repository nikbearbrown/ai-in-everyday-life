# 01. The World Has Changed

*Status: DRAFT, not yet dry-run. Master class, whole room.*

## Executive summary

**What this is.** A 10-minute live demonstration that opens the master class. A student, not the presenter, gives a general-purpose AI a real task, and the room watches it work. Then the presenter asks the question that organizes the whole day.

**Why run it.** The world has changed, and a slide cannot show it. A real task done in under a minute can. The point is not to be amazed. The point is to ask, honestly: *if the first draft is now free, what is left for you?* Students answer by sorting tasks into "hand it over," "supervise it," and "protect it."

**What students leave with.** A concrete picture of what AI does well, a first vocabulary for what stays human, and the sense that this day is about them driving, not watching.

| | |
|---|---|
| **Time** | 10 minutes |
| **Group** | Whole room |
| **Materials** | Projector, one laptop with a general-purpose AI assistant, a stack of "task cards" (below) |
| **Tech** | Wi-Fi. **Fallback below.** |
| **Lead** | Master class lead |

## The two jobs in this module

- **Master it:** Direct it. A student drives the keyboard; the room tells them what to ask.
- **Keep it human:** Name the five human moves out loud and put them on the wall.

## Prep (before the day)

The lead runs the demo task twice in advance and saves both transcripts. **Do not** rely on a lucky run. Whatever happens live, including a stumble, is what the room sees. Saved transcripts are the fallback if Wi-Fi fails, and they are real, not mocked.

## Run of show

1. **Pick a driver (1 min).** A volunteer sits at the laptop. The presenter stands behind them.
2. **The 60-second task (3 min).** The room picks one real, relatable task. Examples: *plan a three-day trip for four friends on a small budget*; *explain how the university admissions process works to a nervous first-year*; *turn these messy notes into a one-page plan.* The driver types. The AI produces a plan, a draft and a table. Let it run. Do not narrate over it.
3. **The question (1 min).** The presenter asks the room: **"If the first draft is now free, what is left for you?"** Take three answers. Write them on a board.
4. **Tier-sort cards (4 min).** Each group of four gets six cards. Sort them into **Hand it over / Supervise it / Protect it**. Defend one card to the room.
   - Format a citation.
   - Draft a first version of an email.
   - Decide which of three plans is worth the risk.
   - Sit with a worried friend.
   - Notice that the question you were asked is the wrong question.
   - Put your name on the result.
5. **Land it (1 min).** Show the five human moves (see [THE-THROUGH-LINE](../THE-THROUGH-LINE.md)). Say: "Today you do both. You get good at driving the tool, and you protect the parts only you can do."

## Say this out loud

"I'm not here to tell you AI is great or AI is dangerous. I'm here to show you that the first draft is free now. What you do next is what makes you worth hiring, worth trusting, and worth listening to."

## Facilitator notes

- The cards have no single right answer. A good debate means the card worked. Push gently on "Hand it over," because handing over is not deleting: *you cannot check what you never learned.*
- Do not claim a number about jobs, speed or adoption. Say only what the room just saw.
- If the AI gets something wrong live, stop. Say "good, that's a catch." That is a free lesson for module 02.

## Unplugged fallback

If there is no Wi-Fi, show the saved transcripts on the projector and run the task cards as written. Say so: "This ran earlier today. Here is what it did."

## Watch-outs

- No personal information in the AI. The task is generic.
- Do not let the demo become the presenter's show. A student drives.
- If the room is shy, pre-brief one student before the session starts.
